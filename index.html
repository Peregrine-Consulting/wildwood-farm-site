Who comes here

Some of this may sound like you.

  • You've been through treatment before, and something about it never quite fit.
  • You're sober, or nearly, and the thought of going back to the apartment where you used is frightening.
  • You've been told to find a higher power by people who don't know what church cost you.
  • Getting sober feels like it might mean losing the only community that ever took you in.
  • Someone you love is queer, using, and running out of time, and you don't know who to call.
  • You're not ready to phone anyone yet. You're just reading.

Any of those is a reasonable place to start a conversation. You don't have to be certain. You only have to be willing to talk.

The distinction that matters

What does queer-normative recovery actually mean?

An LGBTQ+ friendly program accepts you. It has a non-discrimination policy and staff who try not to say the wrong thing. That is the floor, and it is not what you need.

Queer-normative means the program was built around queer experience from the beginning. Your identity is the starting point of the clinical work rather than a complication to be managed. Minority stress, religious trauma, family rupture, and the particular weight of having been unsafe as a child are treated as part of the picture, because for most people here, they are the picture.

In practice it is quieter than it sounds. Nobody spends the first ten minutes of group explaining themselves. Pronouns are the baseline. You can talk about your relationships without translating them.

Questions people actually ask

Before you call.

Will I lose my community if I get sober?

You will probably lose relationships that were built around using, and that is a real loss worth grieving. What people find here is a community where they are known fully and don't have to perform anything. For a lot of queer people those turn out to be the deepest friendships they've had, because they're built on honesty rather than a shared substance.

Is Wildwood only for gay men?

No. Wildwood serves the whole community, including transgender and non-binary residents. Pronouns here are the baseline, not a courtesy that has to be requested.

Do you take insurance?

Sober living itself is private pay, which is true almost everywhere. Outpatient therapy and medication management that happen while you live here can often be billed to insurance separately, so the out-of-pocket figure isn't always the whole picture. We'll walk through it honestly on the phone.

Is it worth relocating to Connecticut for this?

Sometimes. It genuinely helps when your current environment is the trigger, when your whole social network is built around using, or when the programme you need doesn't exist where you live. It works less well when the move itself becomes the plan. We'll tell you honestly if we think staying put is the better call.

What if I'm not ready to commit to six months?

Then say that on the phone. Six months is what the model is built around and we'll explain why, but the first conversation is about whether this is the right fit at all, including the possibility that it isn't, and that somewhere else is.

Talking to us

The first conversation is free, and it is not a sales call.

You can call and ask what a day looks like, whether we've worked with someone in your situation, or what happens if the money runs out. If Wildwood isn't right, we would rather tell you that than admit you.
